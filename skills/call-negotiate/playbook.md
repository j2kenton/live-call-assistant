# Negotiation playbook

The methods `/call-negotiate` coaches from, summarised in our own words from Chris Voss's *Never Split the Difference* (2016) and the later teaching of his firm, the Black Swan Group (its newsletter *The Edge*, courses, interviews). Read the originals for the full picture. Anything marked **(adaptation)** is ours, not Voss's. Why the moves work, and how strong the evidence is for each, is in [theory.md](theory.md).

The [resources](resources/) folder adds practical guides with ready-made moves: [general negotiation](resources/general-negotiation.md), [salary and job offers](resources/salary-and-offers.md), [tech offers from interviewing.io](resources/tech-offers-interviewing-io.md) (leverage, timing, recruiters), [Josh Doody's *Fearless Salary Negotiation*](resources/fearless-salary-negotiation-doody.md) (deflecting, the counter, raises), [Patrick McKenzie](resources/patrick-mckenzie-salary-negotiation.md), [Haseeb Qureshi's ten rules](resources/haseeb-qureshi-ten-rules.md), [Deepak Malhotra's fifteen rules](resources/malhotra-15-rules.md), [principled negotiation](resources/principled-negotiation.md) (*Getting to Yes*, *Getting Past No*), [difficult conversations](resources/difficult-conversations.md), and [the research on first offers](resources/first-offers-research.md). Where they and the book suggest different moves, see [When the approaches differ](#when-the-approaches-differ) below. Your own playbooks, in `~/.live-call-assistant/negotiate/`, take precedence over both.

## The stance

- **Tactical empathy.** Show the other side you understand how they see things and how they feel about them. Understanding isn't agreeing. People who feel heard feel safe, and people who feel safe think more clearly and give more.
- **Aim for "That's right".** When they say it about your summary of their position, they feel understood, and that's when they start to move. "You're right" usually means "please stop talking".
- **"No" is a safe word.** People relax when they're allowed to say no; it protects them. A "no" usually starts the real negotiation rather than ending it. Not every "yes" is real either: there's the counterfeit yes (to get rid of you), the confirmation yes (a reflex) and the commitment yes (a real agreement). Only the last one counts.
- **Listen more than you talk.** Most people treat silence as waiting for their turn. Real listening is hearing what's said and what's behind it. Silence after a label or a question does a lot of the work (Black Swan calls this *dynamic silence*).
- **Voice.** By default, a warm, positive, easy-going voice. For a firm point, the "late-night FM DJ" voice: slow, calm, downward inflection. A pushy, assertive voice almost always backfires.
- **Don't split the difference.** Meeting in the middle often leaves both sides with a bad deal. Look for what each side values differently instead.
- **Look for black swans.** The unknown unknowns: a pressure, constraint or need on their side that would change everything if you knew it. You find them by listening, asking, and noticing what doesn't add up.

## The core tools

| Tool | What it is | Lines to adapt |
| :--- | :--------- | :------------- |
| **Mirror** | Repeat their last one to three words (or the critical ones) as a gentle question, then go quiet. They expand, and you gain time. | "Fixed budget?" … then pause. |
| **Label** | Name their feeling or position, starting with "It seems like", "It sounds like" or "It looks like". Never "I hear you saying" (that makes it about you). Then pause. If it's off, don't defend it: hand it back. | "It seems like this band matters a lot to how the team is paid." |
| **Accusation audit** | Before you ask for something, say out loud the worst things they might be thinking about you or your request. It drains the fear and the resentment out of the room. | "You're probably going to think I'm being greedy, maybe even ungrateful after how generous you've been…" |
| **Calibrated questions** | Open "how" and "what" questions (rarely "why", which sounds accusing). They give the other side the illusion of control while they solve your problem. | "How can we make this work?" "What's the biggest challenge you're facing in this role?" |
| **"How am I supposed to do that?"** | The politest form of "no": it asks them to solve your problem with their offer. | "That's a generous offer, and I appreciate it. How am I supposed to accept that, given where the market is?" |
| **Summary** | Paraphrase their position plus a label, until they say "That's right". | "So the role's been open a while, the team's stretched, and the band was set before the scope grew. Is that right?" |
| **No-oriented questions** | Questions where "no" is the answer that moves things forward. | "Is now a bad time to talk?" "Would it be a ridiculous idea to look at the start date as well?" "Have you given up on filling this role?" (for someone who's gone quiet) |
| **Rule of three** | Get agreement three times in different ways: a yes, a summary they confirm, and a "how" question about making it happen. A real commitment survives all three. | "How will we know it's working at the six-month review?" |

## Reading the other side

- **The three types** (from the book). *Analysts* are methodical and data-driven; silence means they're thinking, so don't fill it, and don't surprise them. *Accommodators* value the relationship, are friendly and chatty, and may agree to things they can't deliver, so check how it will happen. *Assertives* see time as money and want to be heard first; let them talk, mirror and label, and they'll listen afterwards. Most people negotiate as they'd like to be negotiated with, so notice your own type.
- **Pronouns.** People who say "I, me, my" a lot are often not the ones who decide; people who say "we" or "they" about a decision may be pointing at someone off the call.
- **The Pinocchio effect.** People who aren't being straight tend to use more words, and more third-person language, than people who are.
- **Behind the table.** The people who aren't on the call (a manager, finance, a committee) can kill a deal. Ask about them: "How does the rest of the team feel about this?" "What will your manager need to see to sign off?"
- **"Fair".** A powerful word, often used to put you on the defensive ("we've made a fair offer"). Don't defend yourself: label it ("It seems like you feel I'm being unreasonable") or ask "What makes it fair?" You can use it honestly too: early on, "I want you to feel I'm treating you fairly. Stop me at any time if you don't."

## Numbers

- **Bend their reality first.** People hate losing more than they like gaining: frame what's at stake as what they'd lose (the time the role's been open, the cost of starting the search again). An accusation audit before a number softens it. Deadlines are often arbitrary: test them. **Caution from the research** ([theory](theory.md)): people who feel they're losing tend to dig in, so note the cost of no deal gently, and frame your ask as a gain for them.
- **Let them go first** when you can; their first number tells you a lot. If you have to go first, use a range whose bottom is your real goal ("people in roles like this get 180 to 200"), or a precise, non-round number (186,500 sounds calculated; 185,000 sounds like an opening bid).
- **Non-cash terms** are often easier for them to give than money: title, scope, start date, remote days, a signing bonus, equity, training, an early review.
- **The Ackerman model** (for when you're the buyer): set your target, then offer 65%, 85%, 95% and 100% of it, in shrinking steps, with empathy and a different way of saying no between each. End on a precise, non-round number and throw in a small non-cash item to show you've hit your limit. **(Adaptation)** As the seller, mirror it: start above your target and come down in shrinking steps.
- **Don't take yourself hostage.** If they want something you have (your skills, your product), you have leverage, however it looks.

## Salary, offers and raises

From the book and Voss's interviews on pay. These moves fit best where someone has real room to shape the role and the pay: smaller companies, raises, senior hires. At a large company with set levels and bands, the level (and so the band) is set by your interviews, background and experience, not by the recruiter or by how you argue on the call, so these moves mostly work within the band and the package (see [tech offers](resources/tech-offers-interviewing-io.md)).

- **Be pleasantly persistent on non-salary terms.** When the salary really is capped, the role, title and other terms usually aren't, and they can be worth more over time.
- **Negotiate for impact, not just pay.** Voss suggests asking how you can have the most impact possible, and negotiating for the scope and opportunity that brings, which can be worth more than salary over time. Strongest where the role can actually be shaped.
- **Ask what success looks like:** "What does it take to be successful here?" Agree the measures, then ask for a review (and a raise) tied to meeting them.
- **Make them invested in your success.** People who give you advice want to see it work. Asking how to succeed turns a negotiator into a sponsor.
- **Competing offers:** open with an accusation audit ("this may sound disloyal…") and treat it as a problem you're solving together, not a threat.
- **A lowball offer:** label it and use "How am I supposed to accept that?", calmly, in the late-night FM voice.

## When the approaches differ

The sources (the book, the field guides, [interviewing.io](resources/tech-offers-interviewing-io.md) and [Josh Doody](resources/fearless-salary-negotiation-doody.md)) agree on most things: warmth, listening, asking for time, never inventing leverage. Where they differ, there's rarely one right answer: lead with the approach that fits the moment best, and offer the other as an alternative line, so the user can choose.

- **Who names a number first.**
  - **Don't name one before the offer** (interviewing.io, Doody, and the book's preference): deflect, and let their offer set the starting point. Best when you don't know the band, it's early, or they're fishing. Even a number that feels high can be far below their band.
  - **Anchor to the role** (the field guides) when an early number decides the level you're considered for, when the market is well known to you, or when deflecting is costing goodwill: "Based on the scope and responsibilities, my expectations are in the [range]." Then stop. The book's version puts your real goal at the bottom of the range. Either way, the range you say isn't your private floor.
  - **What the research suggests** ([first-offers-research.md](resources/first-offers-research.md)): first offers anchor the result, but the side without market information does better not to open. So deflect before an offer; once there's an offer, or when you're pushed and well informed, anchor with a justified, moderately precise number or a narrow range with your target at the bottom. When they anchor low, think about your target and your value before replying.
- **Live or in writing.** The book values the empathy of a voice call. interviewing.io and Doody counter by email, so you aren't bargaining under pressure and your exact words reach whoever approves. On a live call, the usual best move with a new number is to react warmly, ask for it in writing and a day, and counter afterwards.
- **What a counter looks like.** One specific number 10–20% above the offer, justified by value (Doody); three planned pushes ending in "if you can do Z, I'll sign today", with the target in the middle of a precise range (interviewing.io); the Ackerman steps for buying and one-off prices (the book). For pay, where salary is often capped, move to the package (a scheduled increase, a signing bonus, a review with criteria) rather than haggling in small steps.
- **Competing offers.** interviewing.io treats them as the main leverage, and at large companies with a compensation committee they often are. Doody treats them as optional: your value to them is leverage too. Either way, share only what's true and helps, and never invent or inflate one.
- **One negotiation or several.** interviewing.io lines offers up and negotiates once, at the end; Doody negotiates each offer as it comes. When several processes are running, lining them up is usually stronger.
- **Reasons, or none.** Doody, Malhotra and Qureshi back every ask with reasons; at a large company with levels and bands, a reason rarely changes anything. The skill leaves reasons out by default and suggests one only when someone has room to decide or needs something to take upstairs ([theory](theory.md#the-moves-one-by-one)).
- **Persistence or a decision.** The book leans toward finding a way to yes. The field guides add that no deal can be the right result, and Doody keeps it to two or three asks: if it's truly fixed, or the other side's conduct breaks trust, decide rather than repeat yourself.

## Repair and pace

From the field guides: **one move at a time**, then pause. If you've pushed too hard or worded something clumsily, repair before any tactic: "I'm sorry; I may have put that badly. Can we go back to where this felt workable?" A relationship isn't worth damaging to land one more question, and warmth never means surrendering your boundaries.

## Prepare: the one-page plan

Black Swan's prep sheet (the *Negotiation One Sheet*, from the book's appendix) has five parts. The prep sheet `/call-negotiate` reads ([prep-example.md](prep-example.md)) follows it, with the field guides' private framework added to the goal:

1. **The goal:** your best realistic outcome, specific and written down, with what it's based on (market data, the band, other offers). Voss warns against focusing on your walk-away point (BATNA): it quietly becomes your target. Know it, but plan around the goal.
2. **A summary** of the situation, in a few sentences the other side would agree with.
3. **Labels and an accusation audit**: three to five of each, prepared in advance.
4. **Calibrated questions**: three to five "how" and "what" questions, including some about the people behind the table.
5. **Non-cash items**: what they could give you that costs them little, and what you could give them.
