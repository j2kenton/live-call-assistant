# First offers, anchoring and asking: what the research says

Sources: peer-reviewed negotiation research, summarised in our own words. Key papers: Galinsky & Mussweiler (2001, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*); Mason, Lee, Wiley & Ames (2013, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*); Loschelder et al. (2014, *Psychological Science*; 2016, *Journal of Applied Psychology*; 2016, *Psychological Science*); Maaravi & Levy (2017) and Maaravi, Pazy & Ganzach (2014), *Judgment and Decision Making*; Schweinsberg et al. (2012, *JESP*); Ames & Mason (2015, *JPSP*); Marks & Harold (2011, *Journal of Organizational Behavior*); Bowles, Babcock & Lai (2007, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*); Leibbrandt & List (2015, *Management Science*); Artz, Goodall & Oswald (2018, *Industrial Relations*). Most are lab or survey studies: strong guides, not guarantees.

This turns the playbook's biggest disagreement, who names a number first, into evidence.

## First offers anchor the result

- **The first offer pulls the final outcome toward it.** In experiments, whoever made the first offer usually did better (Galinsky & Mussweiler).
- **The defence when they anchor low:** before replying, think about your own target, your alternatives, and what they'd lose without you. Doing that cancelled the anchor's pull in the same experiments.

## When going first hurts

- **When they know the market and you don't.** The uninformed side does better by not opening (Maaravi & Levy). Before an offer, the employer usually knows the band and the candidate doesn't.
- **When your opener reveals your priorities,** and the other side uses it (Loschelder et al., the information-anchoring model). With money as the only issue, the first-mover advantage holds.

## How high, how precise, ranges

- **Ambitious but defensible.** Extreme openers offend people and cause walk-outs, and even when they win the deal they lower the other side's satisfaction and willingness to work with you (Schweinsberg et al.; Maaravi et al.). 10–20% above the offer sits comfortably inside the defensible zone.
- **Moderately precise beats round.** Precise numbers are stronger anchors, because they suggest you've done the maths (Mason et al.), but experts such as recruiters push back on extreme precision unless there's a reason (Loschelder 2016). "187,500, based on the level and the scope", not "187,437.50".
- **A range with your target at the bottom** drew the best counteroffers without harming the relationship; very wide or unrealistic ranges lose the effect, and the useful ones stayed within about 20% (Ames & Mason).

## Asking at all

- **Negotiating pays, and how matters.** Employees who negotiated earned more; competitive tactics brought the biggest gains, cooperative trade-offs the highest satisfaction, and giving in quickly left people unhappy (Marks & Harold).
- **Gender:** women face more backlash for asking, through being seen as less nice or more demanding (Bowles et al.), and when hours are accounted for, they ask about as often as men but get less (Artz et al.). When an ad says pay is negotiable, the gap in who negotiates disappears (Leibbrandt & List). So: ask. A short reason tied to the role and the team can soften that backlash (Bowles & Babcock 2013).

## What this means in practice

- **Before an offer:** deflect, because the employer has the information advantage.
- **Once there's an offer** (their band is now visible), **or if pushed and well informed:** anchor with one researched, justified, moderately precise number, or a narrow range with your target at the bottom.
- **When they anchor low:** focus on your target and your value before answering.
