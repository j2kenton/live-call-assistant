# Tech job offers: leverage, timing and recruiters (interviewing.io)

Source: interviewing.io, mainly Aline Lerner's posts *How to sabotage your salary negotiation efforts before you even start* and *Exactly what to say when recruiters ask you to name the first number* ([interviewing.io/blog/category/salary-negotiation](https://interviewing.io/blog/category/salary-negotiation)), and their YouTube webinars *Negotiation 101, 201, 301*, *Live Salary Negotiation with an Expert* and *How to Negotiate When You're Screwed* (2022–2024, [youtube.com/@interviewingio](https://www.youtube.com/@interviewingio)). Summarised in our own words. Their figures are self-reported, US big-tech flavoured and partly dated; treat the numbers as rough benchmarks.

Left out on purpose: a few role-play tactics from the videos that rely on deception (claiming an NDA you don't have, an invented decision-maker, reneging on a signed offer). Their own blog says fabricating offers is both unethical and ineffective.

## The core idea: leverage and timing beat phrasing

- **Leverage is real alternatives landing at the same time.** Clever words matter far less than having another offer, or a genuine alternative, when the numbers are discussed.
- **Line the processes up.** Slow some down and speed others up so the offers arrive together. At the onsite, say when you expect to have offers and when you'll decide.
- **Negotiate once, at the end,** ideally with your top choice. Negotiating each offer as it comes reveals your hand, hurries their deadline and tires everyone out.
- **Stay in information-gathering mode until then.** Be warm and enthusiastic, share freely about yourself and your work, and share nothing they can act on: no salary history, no expectations, not where else you're interviewing or how far along, not a pending promotion. Watch for indirect probes, like how much unvested equity you'd leave behind.

## Scripts for the early questions

Each deflection ends with the same reassurance: you won't accept another offer without talking to them first.

- **"What are your salary expectations?" (early):** you don't know enough about the role yet to name a meaningful number; once they make an offer, you'll work with them to make it fit.
- **The same question, later:** the process sounds promising; could they share what a package might look like?
- **"Where else are you interviewing?":** you're talking to a few companies at different stages, and you'll let them know if a deadline comes up. Later: you're wrapping some up and moving others forward, and you'll keep them posted.
- **They volunteer a range:** thank them; you haven't finished researching the market, so you can't comment yet.
- **A timeline question:** giving a rough, honest one (when you expect offers) is fine.
- **You already named a number too early:** things have changed since then: more research, more conversations.

## When the offer comes

- **On the call, don't bargain live.** Spend it on warm rapport. On the money, say it's lower than you hoped but a good starting point, and that you look forward to finding something that works for both sides. Then stop. Ask for the details in writing and a day or two.
- **Counter by email:** short (150–200 words), sent at a good time of day. It gives you time to think, and recruiters do this all day while you don't. Move texts to email.
- **Exploding deadlines are usually softer than they sound.** Show enthusiasm, say the decision matters to you, promise not to drag it out, and propose a specific date.

## Countering

- **Plan three pushes in advance:**
  1. A push with no number: one data point, then "Would it be impossible to meaningfully increase [base / equity]?"
  2. A target: "I'd like to see something closer to [Y]."
  3. A close: "If you can do [Z], I'll sign today."
- **The final number:** put your real target in the middle of a precise, odd range (for example 186,400–189,750); companies often meet in the middle.
- **Rough benchmarks when you know nothing:** 10–20% over the offer on base; a signing bonus around 20% of base; 1.5–2x the equity. Ask for three things at once (base, equity, signing bonus) and expect to get one or two.
- **"What would it take for you to sign today?":** know your answer privately, but out loud ask them to make their best offer, say you'll give it serious consideration, and go quiet.
- **"I'll see what I can do":** stop talking and let them work.

## Recruiter tells

- **"This is final," after one push:** often not. Neither are "it's out of my hands", "one week maximum" or "we don't negotiate"; recruiters regularly extend or move after saying them.
- **"How do you feel about the offer?" or "Does the start date work?":** don't say yes to anything before the pay is agreed.
- **"We need more data" (they want the other offer letter):** decline to send it, and give them a line to pass on instead: they could make this more competitive by raising base to __ and equity to __.
- **Third-party recruiters aren't your advocates.** They earn a share of your first-year base, so a raise barely changes their fee and closing fast matters more to them. Assume what you tell them reaches the company. Once there's an offer, deal with the company's own recruiter.
- **In-house recruiters** mostly push back to avoid extra approvals, not to save money. Pay parity within the team is often their real limit. Ask how they arrived at the offer, and what the band is.

## Other offers, or none

- **Share only what helps.** Share the parts of a competing offer that beat this one (if its cash is higher, compare cash). Don't volunteer details that weaken your case, such as the other offer being from a much smaller company; "another tech company" is enough. Never invent or inflate an offer.
- **No other offers:** use a real alternative ("if I didn't reach X, my plan was to stay and go for promotion"), the unvested equity or bonus you'd leave behind, and market data.

## Level

- **A down-level is a negotiation too.** Say plainly that the level doesn't match, and point to work you've done at the higher level's scope. Ask what fell short, and for a follow-up with the hiring manager or a repeat of the round in question. Raise it at every contact.

## Patterns at large tech companies

Documented for one large US tech company (2024–2025) and reported for others; check what's current.

- **Low first offer, short deadline,** and the recruiter only goes to the compensation committee with a compelling reason, usually a competing offer from a comparable company. Without one, expect little more than a small signing bonus.
- **Team matching buys time:** space out hiring-manager calls and ask to meet engineers on teams you're serious about (but don't slow-play a team that's clearly the one).
- **Hiring managers** are often candid about the real deadline, and can push for exceptions: build rapport and get their email.
- **Recruiters fish for information** ("company X is slow, are you far along there?", "we can connect you with people who chose us over X"): treat these as questions about your leverage.

## Other levers

- **Grow the pie:** turn down a benefit you don't need in exchange for more base; ask whether pay depends on where you live.
- **Talk about money in a relaxed voice,** the way you'd talk about a hobby with a friend.
