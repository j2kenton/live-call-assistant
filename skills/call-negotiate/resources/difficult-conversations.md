# Difficult conversations: colleagues, family and tense moments

Source: Douglas Stone, Bruce Patton and Sheila Heen, *Difficult Conversations: How to Discuss What Matters Most* (Harvard Negotiation Project; 1999, 2nd edition 2010, 3rd edition 2023). Checked against the Harvard Program on Negotiation and Beyond Intractability's summary. Summarised in our own words.

Most useful when the negotiation is with someone you'll keep working or living with, or when emotions are running the conversation. It sits well beside Voss's labels and the field guides' repair lines.

## Three conversations at once

Every hard conversation is really three:

- **What happened:** the facts, who meant what, who's to blame.
- **Feelings:** what each of you feels, said or unsaid.
- **Identity:** what this says about each of you ("am I competent, fair, a good person?").

Notice which one the user is stuck in. An argument about facts is often really about feelings or identity.

## Moves

- **From certainty to curiosity.** Both views can make sense: "I see it differently, and I'd like to understand how it looks from where you are."
- **Intent vs impact.** Don't assume they meant the harm you felt: "I don't think you meant it this way, but it landed as…" When you're the one accused, acknowledge the impact even if you meant well: "I didn't intend that, and I can see it hit you hard."
- **Contribution, not blame.** Map what each person did to get here, and own your part first: "I've probably contributed to this by…"
- **Say feelings plainly,** rather than letting them leak out as judgements: "Honestly, I felt sidelined."
- **Open with the third story.** Describe the gap between your views the way a neutral observer would, then invite them in: "It seems we see the deadline differently. Can we work out why, together?" The best opener when the user starts the conversation.
- **Name the pattern** when you're stuck: "We keep talking over each other. Can we each finish?"
- **Problem-solve together:** ask what would persuade them, say what would persuade you, look for options, agree a fair standard. If nothing works, it's fine to step back and say why.

## For the user, not to say aloud

- **Stay grounded.** Avoid all-or-nothing judgements of yourself ("I'm terrible at this"), expect a strong reaction, and take a break if you need one.
