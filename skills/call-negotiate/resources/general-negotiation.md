# General negotiation playbook

Source: a field guide built from real negotiation practice, with techniques inspired in part by Chris Voss. Original wording and examples. For work, clients, vendors, boundaries and everyday disagreements.

## One-minute card

**Smile.** Stay warm and curious. **Slow down.** Listen and pause. One move at a time.

1. **Understand their view:** "It sounds like [concern]."
2. **Invite more:** "The deadline?" / "Not possible?" [pause]
3. **Make the problem shared:** "**How am I supposed to do that?**" or "How can we make this work for both of us?"
4. **Don't disclose your private limit** just because someone asks for it.
5. **Keep your choice:** a workable agreement is better than winning an argument, and no agreement can be the right result.

**Repair when needed:** "I'm sorry; I may have put that badly. Can we go back to where this felt workable?"

## 1. Define the actual negotiation

Before discussing solutions, write down:

| Question | Your answer |
| :-- | :-- |
| What do I want? | A specific outcome, plus why it matters |
| What may they want? | Pressures, risks, approval constraints, relationship needs |
| What can I trade? | Timing, scope, price, workload, certainty, convenience, risk |
| What is private? | Walk-away point, urgency, concessions, uncommitted alternatives |
| What is my alternative? | What happens if there's no deal? |
| What will I not trade? | Trust, safety, wellbeing, ethics, obligations |

Separate **positions** ("I need it Friday") from **interests** ("I need the material before Monday's launch"). Interests often allow more than one solution.

Don't confuse wanting agreement with needing it, and don't treat the other side's confidence as proof of their leverage.

## 2. The conversational toolkit

**Mirrors** encourage clarification. Repeat the last one to three meaningful words with curious intonation, then pause: "By Friday?" "No flexibility?" "Already approved?" "That's final?" They're useful often, even in consecutive turns: the other person expands without you inventing an explanation for them.

**Labels** show understanding, not agreement: "It sounds like the timing is your main concern." "It seems like you've had to defend this decision internally." "It sounds like you're worried about setting a precedent." A label is a hypothesis they can correct. **Understanding a concern doesn't oblige you to concede.**

**Calibrated how/what questions** are the main problem-solving tool: "How am I supposed to do that?" "How can we make this work?" "What's the biggest obstacle?" "What would need to change?" "What options do we have?" "How can we protect both priorities?" Use real curiosity, not sarcasm. One question at a time. **Ask and wait.**

**No-oriented questions** lower the pressure: "Would it be unreasonable to take another look?" "Would it be a bad idea to try a smaller pilot?" "Would you be opposed to reviewing this next week?" "No" can feel safer than being pushed to agree, but a real "no" can also be final: respect it.

**Accusation audits** address the awkward truth first: "You're probably going to think I'm being difficult, but…" "It might sound like I'm moving the goalposts, but…" "It probably feels like I'm asking for another exception…" Use them only when the feared reaction is plausible. A strong, honest acknowledgement works better than a timid disclaimer.

**Summaries** check you've understood: "So quality can't slip, the date is fixed, and your team is already at capacity. Is that right?" A good summary makes the other side feel heard and surfaces misunderstandings before they become arguments.

**Pace, silence and repair:** a calm, collaborative tone. "Let me think about that." [pause] "I may have said that badly. I'm sorry." "It sounds like I've frustrated you. What am I missing?" **Warmth never means surrendering your boundaries.**

## 3. Common scenarios

| They say | Move |
| :-- | :-- |
| "That's the policy / budget / rule." | "The policy?" [pause] → "What flexibility exists within it?" → "What's the process for an exception?" A real rule may constrain the outcome: learn its scope rather than pretend every rule is negotiable. |
| "I need an answer right now." | "It sounds like you need certainty quickly." [pause] → "What happens if I take until tomorrow?" If you must answer now, choose deliberately; don't improvise agreement under pressure. |
| "If you can't, I'll find someone else." | "Someone else?" [pause] → "It sounds like speed is critical." → "What would a workable handover look like?" Don't threaten back or invent leverage. |
| "Just tell me your bottom line." | "I'd rather look at the whole arrangement first." → "What would help us close the gap?" Share a real boundary when it helps, but don't volunteer private information on reflex. |
| "We already compromised." | "It sounds like you've gone a long way already." [pause] → "What's still hardest to solve?" Acknowledge real movement without treating it as an obligation to accept. |
| "You're being unreasonable." | "You may think I'm asking too much." [pause] → "What part seems hardest to justify?" Stay with the issue rather than defending your character. |
| They get visibly upset. | Stop pressing. Slow down. Label the frustration, own any clumsy wording, and leave room to reset. A relationship isn't worth damaging to land one more tactical question. |

## 4. Examples beyond salary

- **An urgent task when you're already overloaded:** "It sounds like this needs to be done today. I'm already committed to [the other task]. How would you like me to prioritise those two?"
- **A client wants free extra scope:** "It sounds like that feature matters. The current agreement covers the initial scope. How should we adjust scope, cost or timeline to include it?"
- **A vendor won't move on price:** "The price is fixed?" [pause] → "What could you include that would make the package more workable?"
- **A colleague disagrees about a technical decision:** "It sounds like you're worried about [the risk]. What failure modes concern you most? How could we test the options?" If you can't agree, clarify who owns the decision.
- **Family logistics:** "It sounds like the timing is really hard for you. How can we divide this so neither of us is carrying everything?"
- **A trial engagement:** "Would it be a bad idea to start with a short paid pilot, agreed hours, and a review at the end?" Spell out scope, availability, payment and exit terms before doing the work.

## 5. Trade creatively without giving away the essentials

Look beyond the headline demand:

- **Time:** faster delivery, a later start, phased milestones, a review date.
- **Scope:** a pilot first, less now, optional extras priced separately.
- **Price and payment:** fixed fee, retainer, milestones, recurring vs one-off.
- **Risk:** acceptance criteria, warranty, an exit clause, a shared validation plan.
- **Capacity:** a named owner, dedicated time, extra support.
- **Relationship:** a communication rhythm, decision rights, an escalation route.

Make concessions **deliberately**, not to make the conversation feel better. When you give something important, ask for something in return: "I can bring delivery forward if we reduce the scope to [X]. Which matters more to you?"

## 6. Trust, boundaries, and when to leave

A negotiation also shows you how someone treats you **when your interests diverge**. Some friction is normal, but some conduct actively damages trust, and a better number or a pleasant call doesn't automatically repair that.

**A gut feeling is information.** You don't need to prove someone is acting in bad faith to decide an ongoing relationship isn't right for you. Ask yourself:

- Are they engaging with real constraints, or repeatedly dismissing them?
- Does the agreement create clear ownership, authority, resources and accountability?
- Can I live with the risk of this agreement more easily than the risk of walking away?
- Am I negotiating toward something I want, or justifying the effort I've already spent?
- Is more discussion improving the options, or just using up time and energy?

**Decisions need a stopping rule.** Once you've evaluated enough to choose, let the decision stand. Reopen it only for new information that changes the underlying case.

## 7. Quick prep and debrief

**Before (60 seconds):** desired outcome · their likely concern · my real alternative · my non-negotiable boundary · one good calibrated question.

**After (60 seconds):** what did I learn? · what did I agree to, specifically? · what needs written confirmation? · what's the next action, or is it closed?

**Smile. Mirror. Label. Ask how or what. Pause.** An agreement isn't automatically a success, and a principled no isn't a failure.
