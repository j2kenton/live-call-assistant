# Salary and job-offer playbook

Source: a field guide built from real salary negotiations, with techniques inspired in part by Chris Voss. Original wording and examples. Prepare, negotiate, evaluate, decide.

## Two-minute pre-call card

**Smile.** Sound warm, curious, unhurried and genuinely collaborative. **Listen more than you speak.** One concise question, then stop. Mirror freely when it feels natural. Don't reveal your private floor just because someone asks.

- **Anchor to the role** (when you're going first): "I'm excited about the opportunity. Based on the scope and responsibilities, my expectations are in the [range]." **Stop.**
- **Go-to question:** "Given the scope of this role, how am I supposed to make [offer] work?" **Stop.**
- **Fast alternatives:** "The top of the band?" [pause] · "It sounds like another approval would be difficult." · "What would happen if you took the range we discussed to finance?" · "Would it be unreasonable to ask you to try one more time?"
- **If it gets tense:** "I'm sorry; I may have put that badly. Can we go back to where this felt workable?"

**Private rule:** decide your target, acceptable compromises and walk-away factors *before* the call. Salary is one dimension. A deal that negotiates well may still be a job you shouldn't take.

## 1. Qualify the opportunity before the numbers

Salary is partly a **filter**. The budget, level, mandate and immediate priorities show what kind of hire the company thinks it's making. A low budget doesn't prove a bad company or role, but it can reveal a mismatch with the responsibilities and growth you want.

Ask early:

- "What would I actually spend most of my time on in the first three, six and twelve months?"
- "What's funded and approved, and what is the team hoping to do eventually?"
- "When urgent work competes with longer-term projects, who protects the time and adjusts expectations?"
- "When there's a significant disagreement in my area, who makes the final call, and who's accountable?"
- "How has the team's way of working changed recently, and what are you investing in next?"
- "What compensation range is budgeted for this role, and how is it levelled?"

**A job description shows what a company wants someone capable of doing, not what it has committed to let them do.** If a big change is promised, judge the team's capacity to *finish* it, not just start it.

**The direct manager:** notice how they communicate, follow through, react to disagreement, acknowledge your expertise, and protect their team from conflicting priorities. Gut feeling counts when judging a close working relationship.

## 2. Set a private framework

Before the call, decide privately:

| Item | Decide privately |
| :-- | :-- |
| Ideal | The salary and terms you'd be genuinely pleased with |
| Communicated range | A credible range that fits the scope and your goals |
| Acceptable deal | The whole package and working conditions you'd *actually* accept |
| Hard boundaries | Non-salary limits: trust in the manager, workload, role content, authority, wellbeing, location |
| Alternatives | Continuing the search, another process, freelance work, a lower-paid but better-fitting role |

Your **communicated range isn't your private floor**, and you needn't disclose the floor or any financial pressure.

One employer's offer is evidence of what that employer would pay, not a universal market value. Other offers, published ranges and real hiring decisions refine the picture.

## 3. Moves for common moments: choose a move, not a speech

| They say | Move |
| :-- | :-- |
| A low offer | "That figure?" [pause] → "My expectations for this scope are [range]." [pause] → "Given the responsibilities, how am I supposed to make [offer] work?" |
| "What's your minimum?" | "My range for this role is still [range]." Or: "I'd rather see what we can build together than give a minimum in isolation." Don't reveal the floor. |
| "I could *ask* for [number]." | "I appreciate that. My range is still [range]. What would happen if you took that range for consideration?" Don't accidentally endorse a lower number as the new target. |
| "I've *got approval* for [number]." | Appreciate real movement: "We're getting closer. How can we close the remaining gap?" |
| "Finance rejected it." | "Rejected it?" [pause] → "What feedback did they give?" → "Would it be unreasonable to try one more time?" |
| "It's impossible." | "Impossible?" [pause]. Let them clarify. If it really is fixed, explore other terms or decide; don't repeat yourself endlessly. |
| "Take it or leave it." | "Take it or leave it?" [pause] → "It sounds like you need a yes-or-no answer." If needed: "Given the gap, how am I supposed to say yes?" If it's truly final: "I appreciate the clarity. I'll give it some thought." |
| "If I get [number], will you sign?" | Promise only if you truly will. Otherwise: "I appreciate you exploring that. I'd need to consider the whole proposition." Don't invent conditions you don't mean. |
| Frustration or annoyance | Slow down, acknowledge the emotion, repair: "I may have put that badly. I'm sorry. What would make this feel workable again?" |
| An offer you're happy with | "That works for me. I'd be happy to move forward." Make sure every agreed change appears in the written offer. |

More useful lines:

- **Calibrated questions:** "How can we bridge the gap?" "What's making approval difficult?" "What would need to change for this to be approved?" "How does approval work from here?" "How else could we structure the package?"
- **Mirrors:** "Fixed?" "For now?" "Top of the band?" "Already approved?" "Another level?"
- **Labels:** "It sounds like you've worked hard to get this approved." "It seems like you're worried about going back for another exception." "It sounds like there are firm constraints on your side."
- **No-oriented questions:** "Would it be unreasonable to ask you to try one more time?" "Would it be impossible to improve the base salary?" "Would it be a bad idea to explore a guaranteed increase after a year?"
- **Accusation audits:** "You're probably going to think I'm being impossible, but…" "It probably feels like nothing is ever enough, but…" "You may think I'm moving the goalposts, but…"
- **Summaries:** "So the issue is the approved level, not the responsibilities. Have I got that right?" "So that figure isn't approved yet, but you can ask for it?"

**One thought per turn.** Be friendly without apologising for your interests. Never let the urge to fill a silence reveal your floor. An accurate "that's right" matters more than winning an exchange.

## 4. Package levers when the salary is capped

**Prefer guaranteed recurring salary** if long-term pay is the goal. Other levers help but aren't equal substitutes.

1. **A guaranteed scheduled increase,** separate from any signing bonus: the new salary, the exact date, and whether it's automatic. A vague performance review isn't a guarantee.
2. **A signing bonus** to bridge the first year: roughly `12 × (target monthly salary − agreed monthly salary)`, gross. Check when it's paid, the tax, any clawback, and how it affects pension or other contributions.
3. **A formal compensation review** with a date, a target, a decision-maker and objective criteria. A review isn't a raise.
4. **Other terms:** paid leave, sick pay, meals or allowances, flexible or remote work, a training budget, notice terms, or a shorter initial contract.

**Value time realistically.** One extra paid day off a year is worth about one day's pay a year (annual salary ÷ working days in the year), not one day's pay a month.

**Employee vs freelancer:** a freelance rate isn't comparable one-for-one with an employee's gross salary. Allow for benefits, unpaid leave, overheads, admin, tax, insurance, payment risk, and whether you're really free to take other clients. In many places, the label on a contract doesn't by itself decide employment status.

## 5. Evaluate the whole proposition, not the negotiation score

Afterwards, ask:

- **Work:** what will I actually do? Does it build the skills, leadership or domain I want?
- **Authority:** does the responsibility come with real decision rights and an established process, not just a title?
- **Execution:** are the important projects staffed, funded, and protected from constant firefighting?
- **Manager:** do I trust how this person communicates, disagrees, advocates and follows through?
- **Money:** is the recurring pay sustainable and in line with the risk and the upside?
- **Wellbeing:** what's the realistic cost of the job, and of continuing the search?

**Trust isn't a points tally.** Some interactions break trust, and more time or a higher number doesn't necessarily repair it. You're deciding whether you want this manager and this job, not just whether you can win a concession.

**An offer isn't a win if the job is a poor fit.** Decide with the information you have; don't wait for certainty. A new proposal is new information, not an automatic retrial: reopen a decision only if it changes the factors that drove it. A declined offer is still useful market data and practice.

## 6. Lessons to keep

- **Separate intent from impact.** You may not know why someone behaved as they did; you can still decide the relationship isn't right.
- **Separate a budget ceiling from your worth.** "We can't pay that" and "you're not worth that" are different claims.
- **A rejection can be complete and courteous:** no long explanation, no accusation, no implied invitation to reopen unless you want one.
- **Negotiation is information gathering.** How someone handles a real disagreement can tell you as much as the number they approve.
- **Preserve your energy.** Preparation should make you more flexible, not trap you in a long effort to rescue a bad fit.

**After the call (30 seconds):** what was offered · what changed · what I learned about the role and manager · my next action.

**Negotiate thoroughly, but choose deliberately. Your job isn't to make every offer work.**
