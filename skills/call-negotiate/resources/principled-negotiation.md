# Principled negotiation: Getting to Yes and Getting Past No

Sources: Roger Fisher, William Ury and Bruce Patton, *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In* (3rd edition, 2011); William Ury, *Getting Past No: Negotiating in Difficult Situations* (revised edition, 1993). Checked against the publishers' pages, the Harvard Program on Negotiation ([pon.harvard.edu](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/)) and Beyond Intractability's summaries. Summarised in our own words.

This is the other foundational school beside Voss: calmer and more analytical. Much of it complements the rest of the playbook; where it differs, offer it as an alternative (see the end).

## Getting to Yes: four principles

- **Separate the people from the problem.** When it turns personal, move both of you to the same side of the table: "Let's look at this together: what would a good outcome look like for both of us?"
- **Interests, not positions.** Ask what's behind a demand, and why they don't want the obvious alternative: "What would that number give you? What's driving it?" (Use "what", not "why": "why" can sound accusing.)
- **Invent options before deciding.** Separate brainstorming from committing, and look for things that cost one side little and are worth a lot to the other: "Can we list a few options before we judge any of them? Nobody's committing yet." Useful when the talk is stuck on one number.
- **Insist on objective criteria.** Market data, precedent, professional norms, a fair procedure. Test a standard by asking whether both sides would accept being bound by it. Yield to principle, never to pressure: "What's that figure based on? Here's the benchmark I'm using." If you can't agree on a standard, agree on a fair way to decide.

## When they won't play

- **Negotiation jujitsu:** don't push back. Don't attack their position; look behind it ("Help me understand how you got there"). Don't defend your idea; invite criticism ("What's wrong with this from your side?"). Treat a personal attack as an attack on the problem. Ask a question, then stay quiet.
- **One draft, improved together:** instead of trading positions, write one draft that both sides keep criticising and revising: "Let me put something on paper, and you tell me what's wrong with it."
- **Dirty tricks** (an extreme opening, new demands after each concession, good cop/bad cop, take-it-or-leave-it): recognise the tactic, name it calmly, and negotiate the rules: "I notice the ask has grown since we agreed X. Can we agree how we're handling changes?" Ask to verify claims without calling anyone a liar.

## Getting Past No: five steps for difficult counterparts

1. **Go to the balcony.** Don't strike back, give in or walk out. Pause, remember what you want, spot their tactic, and don't decide on the spot: "Let me make sure I've understood: you're saying…", or "I'd like to sleep on that and come back to you tomorrow."
2. **Step to their side.** Listen, acknowledge their points and feelings, agree wherever you honestly can, and say "yes, and" rather than "but": "You're right that timing matters, and I also need…"
3. **Reframe.** Treat their position as one possible way to meet their interests. Ask "what if" questions, and recast an attack as a concern: "What if we tried X? What would make that unworkable for you?"
4. **Build a golden bridge.** Make it easy for them to say yes: ask for their ideas, offer choices, help them save face, give them credit, move in small steps ("nothing's final until everything's agreed"): "What would make this easy to take back to your manager?"
5. **Use power to educate, not to force.** Reality-test instead of threatening: "What do you see happening if we can't agree?" Let them know your alternative as information, not a threat, and pair it with a way back to the table.

## Where it differs from Voss: offer as alternatives

- **Your alternative (BATNA).** The book makes improving and knowing your best alternative to a deal central. Voss warns that it quietly becomes your target. Use it as private preparation for when to walk away; rarely say it, and if you do, say it calmly: "I do have other options, but I'd much rather make this work."
- **Objective criteria vs emotion first.** A criteria-based line ("What's that based on?") is a calmer alternative to a label or an anchor, and strong against "that's our policy". Voss warns that "fair" can be used as a weapon, so ground it in a specific standard.
- **Building agreement vs "no".** Ury builds small agreements ("yes, and"); Voss distrusts easy yeses and prefers questions where "no" moves things forward. Both work; offer the one that suits the person.
