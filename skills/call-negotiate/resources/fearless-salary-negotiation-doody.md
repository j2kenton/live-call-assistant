# Fearless Salary Negotiation (Josh Doody)

Source: Josh Doody, *Fearless Salary Negotiation*, and his articles at [fearlesssalarynegotiation.com](https://fearlesssalarynegotiation.com/) (the salary negotiation guide, salary-expectations question, email samples, competing offers, expiring offers, lowball offers, benefits, minimum acceptable salary, recruiters, raises). Summarised in our own words.

His style is direct: no labels or mirrors, a single number backed by value. Competing offers help but aren't required: your value to them is the leverage.

## Before the process

- **Set your walk-away number (lowest acceptable base) before the first interview,** from your current pay, your market value, what people in the role earn, and how much they need you versus how much you need this job. Change it only for real new information.
- **Keep your current pay to yourself.** Leave salary fields on forms blank where you can.

## Deflecting "What are your expectations?" or "What do you earn now?"

Escalate only as far as they push:

1. You'd rather focus on the value you'd bring; you don't have a fixed number; they know what the role is worth, and you're looking for a real step up. Pushed again: you'd like to hear what they think is fair.
2. They say they need to check you're in range: offer to tell them whether *their* range is in the ballpark if they share it. That commits you to nothing.
3. **The last resort:** you'd rather not share your current employer's private pay information, just as they wouldn't want their employees to.

A company that threatens to stop the process over this is showing you something about itself.

## When the offer arrives

- Show enthusiasm, don't react to the numbers, and ask for a day or two to think it over. Ask them to email the details as a list.
- **"Sounds like we have a deal?":** not yet; you'd like a little time to look at it properly. Accepting and declining aren't the only options: negotiating is the third.

## The counter

- **One specific number, never a range.** 10% above the offer if you're cautious, up to 20% if your position is strong. If offer + 10% is still below your walk-away number, counter at your walk-away number.
- **Justify it with value, not need:** three to seven reasons you'll help their team reach its goals. Not your current pay.
- **In writing,** so the recruiter can forward your exact words to whoever approves it. Calm wording: "I'd be more comfortable at [X]."
- **A lowball offer** (far below your walk-away) from a company that clearly wants you: first ask them to improve it, without giving a number.

## Their reply: plan your "negotiation window" in advance

Their answer often comes by phone, quickly. Decide every step before it happens:

- **They agree:** accept.
- **They come back a bit lower:** you'd sign at one step higher.
- **Base won't move:** ask for your top non-salary item, framed as "if you can do this, I'm ready to sign", then your second, then your third.
- **They ask how you got your number:** a few reasons about your value; never your salary history.
- **Unsure:** listen, ask a question if needed, and ask for a day.
- **Walk-away number as an ultimatum:** only at the very end, and only if you mean it.

**Benefits come after base is settled, one at a time, in your order of priority.** Roughly from cheapest for them to dearest: flexible schedule, relocation, signing bonus, equity, extra leave, salary. Bonuses you'd forfeit by leaving are a good reason for a signing bonus.

## When to stop

- **The first counter does most of the work.** Keep to two or three asks in total, to protect the relationship.
- If they've gone quiet for two or three business days, send a light check-in without asking what they think of your counter.
- Stop once an ask is accepted. You can still decline: being willing to walk away is the leverage.

## Expiring offers

Deadlines aren't necessarily fake. Negotiate first, then ask for the time you need, and don't threaten to walk away unless you would.

## Raises

- **Build the case on results:** revenue gained, costs cut, work sped up. Keep a running log of wins and praise.
- **Ask in a one-to-one,** then follow up in writing: time since your last adjustment, the market midpoint for your role, your target, your results.
- **Raise budgets are set before review time,** so ask early in the cycle.
- **"Not now":** ask for specific goals and a date, so it can't drift ("What would I need to show by [date] for this to happen?").
