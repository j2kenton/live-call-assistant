# The psychology behind the moves

Why the playbook's moves work, how strong the evidence is for each, and when they backfire. For practice, preparation, and when the user asks "why?". During a live call, one line from here is plenty.

Summarised in our own words from peer-reviewed research (papers named in each section) and, where it fits, Chase Hughes's behaviour and influence work, labelled as practitioner material.

The moves work when they're done well and can backfire when applied mechanically: each section says how.

## Evidence labels

- **Strong:** many studies or meta-analyses agree.
- **Moderate:** good studies, but few, small or mostly in labs.
- **Weak or mixed:** findings conflict, or don't replicate well.
- **Untested:** sensible and widely taught, but no direct test found. The idea behind it may be well supported.
- **Contradicted:** tested, and the evidence goes against it.

## The principle that ties it together: sincere works, detected technique backfires

- **People who spot an influence tactic reinterpret everything you said as manipulation.** Persuasion knowledge model (Friestad & Wright 1994); flattery read as insincere when the motive is obvious (Campbell & Kirmani 2000); ingratiation works less when it's transparent (Gordon 1996). **Moderate.** This is why people who "tried the techniques" report them backfiring.
- **Faked emotion backfires; felt emotion doesn't.** In negotiation experiments, displayed anger that wasn't felt raised distrust and made the other side demand more; genuinely felt anger won concessions (Côté, Hideg & van Kleef 2013). **Moderate.**
- **So the test for every move:** would you be comfortable if they knew exactly what you were doing and why? A label you mean, a question you want answered, a reason you actually have, all pass. A script delivered to "trigger" someone doesn't.

## The moves, one by one

**Labels** ("It sounds like the band is a real constraint")
- *Why:* naming an emotion reduces its intensity for the person naming it (affect labelling: Lieberman et al. 2007; Torre & Lieberman 2018 review). **Moderate.** Feeling understood lowers defensiveness and opens people up (Reis, Lemay & Finkenauer 2017; high-quality listening: Itzchakov & Kluger; Kluger et al. 2023 meta-analysis). **Moderate.**
- *Caveat:* the brain research is about labelling your *own* feelings. Labelling someone else's out loud and calming them is **untested** as such: it works, if it does, mainly through feeling understood.
- *Backfires when:* the label is wrong and delivered as a verdict, or sounds scripted. Offer it as a guess they can correct, and if it's off, don't defend it: apologise lightly and hand it back. "Sorry, I may have misread that. How would you put it?" Done well, even a wrong label gets them talking; done poorly, it's worse than saying nothing.

**Mirrors** (repeating their last few words as a question)
- *Why:* mimicry is linked with liking and trust; in one negotiation experiment, mimicking negotiators reached more deals (Maddux, Mullen & Galinsky 2008); matching language early in online negotiation helped (Swaab, Maddux & Sinaceur 2011). **Weak or mixed:** the correlations are solid, but several experiments found no rapport effect from being mimicked, and the negotiation findings haven't been independently replicated.
- *Practical reading:* a mirror doesn't make anyone agree to anything. Its value is connection (you're using their words) and an open door to say more, which pairs naturally with silence.
- *Backfires when:* it's noticeable or mechanical, especially several in a row.

**Calibrated "how/what" questions, no-oriented questions, "How am I supposed to do that?"**
- *Why:* people push back when their freedom to choose feels threatened (psychological reactance: Brehm 1966; Rains 2013 meta-analysis). **Moderate.** Questions leave the choice with them. Adding "but you're free to say no" to a request increased compliance in many studies (Carpenter 2013), but the effect shrinks to near zero in the most rigorous ones (a later re-analysis). **Weak or mixed.**
- *Caveat:* Voss's specific formulations are **untested**; the autonomy idea behind them is well founded.

**Accusation audits** ("You're probably going to think I'm asking for a lot…")
- *Why:* disclosing a negative before the other side raises it makes it count for less ("stealing thunder": Williams, Bourgeois & Croyle 1993), and warning about an objection builds resistance to it (inoculation: Banas & Rains 2010). Two-sided messages that answer the other view beat one-sided ones (O'Keefe 1999). **Moderate** for the mechanisms; **untested** in negotiation.
- *Backfires when:* it puts a negative in their head that wasn't there, or it's overdone into self-deprecation. Use it only for reactions they're likely to have anyway.

**Summaries until "That's right"**
- *Why:* feeling accurately understood predicts trust and openness, and good listening makes people's positions less extreme (see Labels). **Moderate.** The specific "that's right" milestone is **untested**.

**Silence after a question or an offer**
- *Why:* in negotiation experiments, pauses of three seconds or more led to more creative, mutually better deals, by shifting both sides into a more deliberate, less "fixed pie" mindset (Curhan et al. 2022). **Moderate,** one paper.
- *Caveat:* silence created value; it didn't increase the user's share. When status was salient, it helped only the higher-status side.
- *Use it on purpose:* after your own label or question, silence invites them to fill it. After their offer, or in place of an answer they're waiting for, it reads as hesitation. That can be exactly what you want: a pause after an offer sometimes brings a better one without a word said (practitioners call it the flinch; **untested**). Just know how it reads, and use it when hesitation serves you, not by accident.

**Giving reasons**
- *The research:* in the classic "because" study, a reason raised compliance (Langer, Blank & Chanowitz 1978); for bigger requests, good reasons helped more than weak ones, and the "any reason works" finding didn't replicate well (Folkes 1985). **Moderate** that good reasons help with everyday requests; untested for salary offers.
- *The coaches disagree:* Doody, Malhotra and Qureshi say to back every ask with reasons; others say a reason rarely moves anything.
- *At a large company with set levels and bands, a reason usually doesn't matter.* The offer follows the level, and the level follows your interviews, background and experience, all settled before the call. The recruiter mostly works within rules and the band. Arguing that you deserve more, or asking for a bigger remit, won't move you to another level. What moves a number within the level is mostly leverage (a competing offer) and the package, not a case for yourself.
- *So the skill doesn't suggest reasons by default.* It suggests one only when it would help: when someone has real room to decide (a smaller company, a raise, a contract), or when the recruiter needs something to take to the compensation committee (usually a competing offer). Even then, keep it short.
- *A weak reason is low-risk:* it isn't a lie, and if they push back ("so what?"), let it go lightly ("Fair enough, it's not a big deal") and move on.

**First offers and anchors:** see [resources/first-offers-research.md](resources/first-offers-research.md). **Strong** that first offers anchor; **moderate** for when going first hurts, precision, and ranges.

**Framing as a loss** (Voss's "bend their reality")
- *Why:* losses loom larger than equal gains (prospect theory: Kahneman & Tversky 1979). **Strong** in general, though its size is debated (Gal & Rucker 2018; Brown et al. 2024 estimate losses weigh about twice as much).
- *Caveat, important:* in negotiation experiments, people thinking in losses held out and risked impasse; people thinking in gains conceded more and settled (Neale & Bazerman 1985). **Moderate.** So framing *their* side as a loss can make them dig in. Safer: describe your ask as a gain for them ("you'd have the role filled this month"), and keep loss framing for gently noting the cost of no deal.

**Reciprocity and concessions**
- *Why:* people feel obliged to return concessions, and matched concessions are a robust bargaining norm (Pruitt 1981; Cialdini et al. 1975). **Strong** for reciprocity. The "big request first, then the real one" tactic raises agreement slightly but not follow-through (Feeley, Anker & Aloe 2012). **Weak.**
- *In practice:* make concessions deliberately and visibly, and ask for something back.

**Fairness**
- *Why:* people reject offers they see as unfair even at a cost to themselves, driven by anger at the unfairness (ultimatum game: Güth et al. 1982; Pillutla & Murnighan 1996). Being treated fairly in the *process* predicts satisfaction and keeping to the deal (Colquitt et al. 2001; Hollander-Blumoff & Tyler 2008). **Strong.**
- *In practice:* this is why "fair" is such a loaded word, why objective criteria persuade, and why how you negotiate matters as much as what you get.

**Rapport and small talk**
- *Why:* rapport built at the start increases cooperation (Drolet & Morris 2000); a short personal call before an email negotiation cut impasses from 38% to 9% (Nadler 2004). **Moderate.**

**Voice** (calm, slower, lower)
- *Why:* lower-pitched voices are heard as more dominant (Puts et al. 2006) and preferred in leaders (Klofstad et al. 2012), and people unconsciously signal and read rank through voice in negotiations (Ko, Sadler & Galinsky 2015). **Moderate** for how voice is perceived. Voss's specific "late-night FM DJ" downward inflection is **untested**. Not something the skill can see from a transcript.

**Reading the other side**
- *Pronouns:* lower-status people tend to say "I" more, higher-status people "we" and "you" (Kacewicz et al. 2014). **Moderate,** but small effects about status, not proof of who decides.
- *Lies:* people judge lies at about 54% accuracy, barely above chance, and experts aren't much better (Bond & DePaulo 2006); most behavioural cues are weak (DePaulo et al. 2003). **Strong.** Voss's "Pinocchio effect" (liars use more words and third-person pronouns) comes from one text-chat study (Van Swol, Braun & Malhotra 2012) and conflicts with wider findings. **Weak.**
- *In practice:* never coach the user to judge that someone is lying. Ask a calm question that verifies instead ("How would that work in the written offer?").

## Chase Hughes's frameworks: where they fit

Chase Hughes's books (*The Ellipsis Manual*, *Six-Minute X-Ray*, *The Behavior Operations Manual: Neuro-Cognitive Intelligence*) and his NCI University courses package many ideas into named models. The models haven't been validated as models, but several rest on solid research. The skill uses the parts that fit honest negotiation, in the open.

| Idea | Evidence | How the skill uses it |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **FATE:** focus, authority, tribe, emotion as the levers of attention and decision | Untested as a model; its parts rest on solid research (novelty and attention, authority, in-group belonging, emotion in decisions) | *Authority* = calm delivery, real alternatives and objective criteria, not status games. *Tribe* = shared goals and honest "we" language ("we both want this role filled well"). *Emotion* = labels. *Focus* = one clear point at a time. |
| **Needs map and decision map:** which needs or decision drivers dominate a person | Untested | Not profiling. Ask about priorities openly ("What matters most to you in filling this?") and speak to what they say: interests, as in *Getting to Yes*. |
| **Word choice:** pronouns, hedges | Moderate for status, small effects | Notice "I" versus "we" as a hint about who decides, then ask. |
| **Authority in your own delivery** | Moderate (hedges and over-apologising read as powerless) | Fewer hedges, no over-apologising, calm pace. Warm and steady. |
| **Behaviour changes** | A heuristic at best. As Hughes himself cautions, a change outside means something is going on inside, not what: discomfort about the offer, boredom, a meeting they're late for, anything | On its own it means little; no rule follows from it. It counts only when it fits other signs (what they've said, a pattern across the call), as one small piece of the picture. Never a conclusion. |

NLP adds nothing here that the playbook doesn't already cover better (reframing is in *Getting Past No*; its precision questions are calibrated questions), so the skill doesn't use it.

## Moves derived from the principles

Not from any one source: built from the research above. Marked as derived when suggested.

- **Frame your ask as their gain** (gain framing): "If we can get to X, you'd have this role filled this week and I could start on [the project]."
- **Ask for their expertise** (status and the golden bridge): "You know these bands far better than I do. If you were in my position, what would you do?"
- **Count three seconds** after their offer before answering (silence).
- **A few minutes of real small talk** before the money, especially before an email exchange (rapport).
- **Hedge labels, not positions** (feeling understood without overconfidence; powerless speech): "It sounds like…, is that fair?" for their feelings; plain, unhedged statements for your own ask.
- **Make concessions visible and conditional** (reciprocity): "I can be flexible on the start date. Could you move on the signing bonus?"

## Out of bounds

The skill never suggests: covert techniques (embedded commands, hypnotic patterns, conditioned emotional "anchors"), profiling someone to play on their fears, faked emotions, judging someone a liar from their behaviour (people are barely better than chance at it, and getting much better isn't worth the effort for most people), flattery or complaint-baiting used to extract information, reading thinking styles from eye movements or word choice, or invented offers, deadlines or decision-makers. Besides being dishonest, these are exactly the moves most likely to be spotted, and to cost the user the deal.
